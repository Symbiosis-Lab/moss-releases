# William Blake: Creative Philosophy and Visual Style

## Text and Image as Inseparable Unity

Blake did not see text and image as separate things. They were one unified artistic act — what scholar W.J.T. Mitchell called "composite art." Mitchell argues that in Blake's illuminated poems, "neither the graphic nor the poetic aspect consistently predominates: their relationship is more like an energetic rivalry, a dialogue between vigorously independent modes of expression."

The image does not illustrate the poem, and the poem does not give a verbal expression of the image — they are two parts that together create the whole experience. They exist in dialectical relationship, not subordination.

Three styles of illumination appear across his works:
1. An illustrative mode where designs support text
2. An independent/assertive mode where designs tell a parallel story
3. A complex iconographic mode where pictures play structural and symbolic roles

Blake composed text and image simultaneously on the copper plate, deciding placement, letter size, line spacing, and illustration extent during execution. He often did not know beforehand which lines would go on which plate.

### Visual-Textual Integration Techniques

- **Text embedded within images**: In "Infant Joy," the poem is nestled between flower stems, literally growing from the plant
- **Text structured by images**: In "The Tyger," a bare tree's branches divide the six stanzas
- **Interlinear decoration**: Tiny figures, insects, birds, vines, and tendrils inhabit the spaces between lines
- **Letters becoming vegetation**: On the Songs of Innocence title page, the italic letters "burst into flame-like vegetation"
- **Inspiration from embroidered samplers** — but Blake went further, eliminating strict framing to make text and decoration equals

## Core Philosophy

### Imagination as Supreme Faculty

- "Imagination is the real and eternal world of which this vegetable universe is but a faint shadow."
- "The whole Business of Man Is The Arts & All Things Common."
- "If the doors of perception were cleansed every thing would appear to man as it is, Infinite."

### The System Quote in Context

"I must Create a System, or be enslav'd by another Man's; I will not Reason & Compare: my business is to Create." — spoken by Los (the imaginative faculty) in *Jerusalem*. Meaning: one must develop one's own way of understanding through creative action, not adopt systems created by others.

### The Doctrine of Contraries

"Without Contraries is no progression." Blake believed opposites are creative tensions necessary for life. "Energy is Eternal Delight." Innocence and Experience are not moral categories but complementary energies.

### Fourfold Vision

1. Single Vision / "Newton's sleep" — purely rational, spiritual blindness
2. Twofold — adds emotion
3. Threefold — imagination awakens, symbolism emerges
4. Fourfold — mystical, direct experience of eternity

"May God us keep / From Single vision & Newton's sleep!"

### Opposition to Reynolds

Blake's annotations to Reynolds' *Discourses* are savage: "To Generalize is to be an Idiot; To Particularize is the Alone Distinction of Merit." Against Reynolds' view that genius is acquired through study: "Man is Born Like a Garden ready Planted & Sown. This World is too poor to produce one Seed."

### Other Essential Quotes

- "The road of excess leads to the palace of wisdom."
- "The tygers of wrath are wiser than the horses of instruction."
- "Eternity is in love with the productions of time."
- "Energy is the only life, and is from the Body; and Reason is the bound or outward circumference of Energy."

## The Relief Etching Method

### Why Not Moveable Type?

Conventional 18th-century book production divided labor: text set in one shop, illustrations engraved in another, assembled together. This was antithetical to Blake's vision. Relief etching let him compose directly, autographically, as though the copper plate were paper — "freed him to think in new ways, to unite invention and execution."

### The Six-Step Process

1. Polished copper to mirror finish
2. Wrote text backward and drew illustrations with acid-resistant varnish
3. Poured diluted nitric acid (45-90 minutes) — acid bit away unprotected metal
4. Applied warm relief-printing ink with linen dabbers
5. Ran through rolling press with dampened paper
6. Hand-colored each copy with watercolors — no two copies alike

### The Lettering

Blake favored italic script because it was easier to write backward. His calligraphic text is never typographic. The italic letters of "Songs of Innocence" are described as "alive, bursting into flame-like vegetation," while the roman capitals of the Experience title page echo "dead bodies."

### The Visionary Justification

Blake credited the method to a vision of his deceased brother Robert. He called it "printing in the infernal method, by corrosives... melting apparent surfaces away, and displaying the infinite which was hid."

## William and Catherine Blake

Catherine Boucher was illiterate when she married William in 1782. He taught her to read, write, and engrave.

- She pulled proof impressions and printed both relief and intaglio plates
- She operated the rolling press while Blake managed inking
- She was an active participant in hand-coloring with artistic autonomy — "neither he nor Mrs. Blake copied the other exactly"
- She managed drying, assembled pages, bound leaves

Gilchrist: "The poet and his wife did everything in making the book — writing, designing, printing, engraving — everything except manufacturing the paper: the very ink, or colour rather, they did make."

## Visual Style

### Songs of Innocence (1789)
- Colors: delicate, light. Early copies in earth tones (yellow ochre, raw sienna). Printed in brown ink.
- Vegetation: lush, abundant, curving. Flowering plants spiraling organically.
- Lines: flowing, sinuous, finely detailed with clear textures.
- Mood: pastoral, open, protective. Visual equivalents of divine care.
- Figures: children, lambs, angels in pastoral settings.

### Songs of Experience (1794)
- Colors: deeper, more saturated. Printed in orange and black inks.
- Vegetation: bare branches, dead trees, skeletal forms.
- Lines: bolder outlines, less textural detail.
- Mood: dark, constricted, oppressive. Fractured spaces.
- Figures: hunched old men, suffering bodies, diseased forms.

### The Decorative Vocabulary
Vines and tendrils coiling from text; trees (lush in Innocence, bare in Experience); flames (especially in Experience and Prophetic Books); tiny human figures dancing between text lines; birds, insects, worms in interlinear spaces; serpents weaving through headings; flowers both realistic and fantastic; atmospheric clouds via watercolor washes.

### Key Works

**The Ancient of Days** (Europe a Prophecy, 1794): Urizen kneeling within an incomplete red circle, surrounded by fiery light. His compass extends into darkness below. Musculature influenced by Michelangelo. Blake's "singular favourite" — he was coloring a copy on his deathbed.

**The Tyger** (Songs of Experience): A remarkably calm tiger walks past a bare tree. The tiger looks serene, almost smiling — deliberately contradicting the poem's fierce language. This visual-textual dissonance enacts Blake's doctrine of contraries.

**Infant Joy** (Songs of Innocence): Two flowers bloom from grass. The poem is nestled between flower stems. Flowers resemble both tulips and flames.

## Blake as Independent Creator

His 1793 Prospectus declared a method that combined "the Painter and the Poet" at "less than one fourth of the expense." He explicitly rejected subscriptions: "No Subscriptions are asked, for none are wanted; but the Author will produce his works, and offer them to sale at a fair price."

He had "the temperament of the entrepreneur, continually willing to venture whatever little capital he had." But commercial reality was harsh: *Songs of Innocence and Experience* exists in only about 24 copies.
