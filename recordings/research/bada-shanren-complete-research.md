# Research Report: 八大山人 (Bada Shanren / Zhu Da, 1626-1705)

## Summary

This is a comprehensive research report on Bada Shanren, one of China's greatest painters. The research covers his life timeline, names and seals, surviving works, artistic philosophy, relationships with other artists, political symbolism, famous works, and actual quotes/inscriptions. This report is intended to inform the design of a website dedicated to his legacy.

## 1. Life Timeline and Key Periods

### Birth and Royal Lineage (1626)

Born **Zhu Tongxu** (朱统𨨗), later known as **Zhu Da** (朱耷), in 1626 in Nanchang, Jiangxi Province. He was the **ninth-generation descendant of Zhu Quan** (朱权), the seventeenth son of Ming dynasty founder Zhu Yuanzhang. His ancestral hometown was Fengyang County, Anhui -- the same as the founding emperor's.

His family was accomplished in the arts:
- **Grandfather Zhu Duozheng** (朱多正): poet, painter, calligrapher, and seal-carver
- **Father Zhu Moujin** (朱谋𪇼): a painter and calligrapher who was **mute from birth** (a detail that haunts the son's later silence)

Bada received a classical education and was a child prodigy -- began studying poetry at age 7, painting and calligraphy from childhood. He passed the **first-level civil service examination** (秀才) in the early 1640s. He married in his late teens and had at least one child.

### Fall of the Ming (1644-1645)

In 1644, Li Zicheng's rebel army took Beijing, and the last Ming emperor **Chongzhen committed suicide** by hanging himself on Coal Hill (煤山). The Manchu Qing forces then swept south. **Nanchang was occupied in 1645.** Bada was 18-19 years old.

The Qing systematically **purged and executed members of the Zhu imperial family.** Bada's wife and father died during this period (exact circumstances debated). Fearing for his life as a direct Ming descendant, he fled to the mountains.

### Buddhist Monastic Period (1648-c.1680, approximately 30 years)

Around 1648 (age ~23), Bada **shaved his head and became a Buddhist monk** at a temple west of Nanchang. His Buddhist name was **Chuanqi** (传綮), with style name **Ren'an** (刃庵).

He studied **Chan (Zen) Buddhism**, specifically the **Caodong** (曹洞) and **Linji** (临济) sects. He proved a formidable teacher:

> "住山二十年，从学者常百余人" (Lived in the mountain for twenty years, with over a hundred students regularly studying under him.)

He rose to become an **abbot** (宗师), addressed by friends as **Xuege** (雪个) or **Abbot Xuege** (雪个上人). During these years, he studied the calligraphic masters (Ouyang Xun, Dong Qichang, Huang Tingjian) and painted as a "hobby" -- only **11 of his surviving works** date from this period (1659-1678).

Key monastic-period works:
- **Flower Studies album** (花卉册)
- **Vegetable and Fruits handscroll**
- **Lotus album** (c. 1665) -- 8 leaves, bears the seals of his Buddhist names Chuanqi, Ren'an, and Fajue
- **Ink Flowers handscroll** (墨花图卷, 1666)

### Daoist Period at Qingyunpu (c.1662-c.1680)

This is a complex and somewhat overlapping period. Around 1662 (age ~36), when Qing rule stabilized in the early Kangxi period, Bada returned to Nanchang and became the **founder/restorer of the Qingyunpu Daoist Temple** (青云谱道院) -- a site where his ancestor Prince Zhu Quan had once practiced Daoism. He lived there with his brother **Niu Shihui** (牛石慧) for over twenty years as a **Daoist priest** (道人).

The Qingyunpu temple (now the **Bada Shanren Memorial Museum** 八大山人纪念馆 in Nanchang) was his primary residence during this long period. The transition from Buddhist monk to Daoist priest reflects both his spiritual searching and possibly his practical need for a safer identity.

### Mental Breakdown / "Madness" (c.1678-1680)

In the late 1670s, Bada grew increasingly dissatisfied with monastic life and sought relationships outside the monastic order. **Hu Yitang** (胡亦堂), the magistrate of Linchuan (临川), invited him to stay at his official residence and help write the local gazetteer.

After about a year in Linchuan (c.1679-1680), Bada **suffered a dramatic breakdown**. The biography by **Shao Changheng** (邵长蘅, 1637-1704) is our primary source:

> "忽忽不自得，遂发狂疾，忽大笑、忽痛哭竟日。" (Suddenly restless and unsettled, he fell into madness -- now bursting into great laughter, now into bitter weeping, all day long.)

> "一夕，裂其浮屠服，焚之，走还会城。" (One evening, he tore his monastic robes, burned them, and walked [120 km] back to Nanchang.)

After arriving in Nanchang:

> "独自徜徉市肆间，常戴布帽，曳长领袍，履穿踵决，拂袖翩跹行。市中儿随观哗笑，人莫识也。" (He wandered alone through the marketplace, wearing a cloth cap, dragging a torn long robe, with worn shoes showing bare heels, swinging his sleeves as he walked. Children in the market followed him laughing and jeering; no one recognized him.)

He then wrote the character **"哑"** (dumb/mute) on a large piece of paper and hung it on his door, refusing to speak to anyone. But:

> "善笑...喜饮益甚" (He loved to laugh... and drank even more than before.)

> "赌酒胜则笑哑哑，数负则拳胜者背，笑愈哑哑不可止，醉则往往唏嘘泣下。" (When he won at drinking games, he laughed with a hoarse "ya ya"; when he lost repeatedly, he would pat the winner's back, laughing even harder, unable to stop. When drunk, he would often sigh and weep.)

**Was the madness real or feigned?** Scholars are divided. Many believe it was a deliberate strategy to avoid political persecution and the forced collaboration that Qing officials demanded of talented scholars. His nephew recognized him and took him in; after "久之" (a long time) -- about 2-3 years -- his condition improved.

### Professional Painter Period (1680-1705)

Around 1680, at approximately age 55, Bada **left the priesthood permanently** and refashioned himself as a **professional painter, calligrapher, and poet.** He remarried (his second marriage) but it was unhappy, and he soon became single again. His paintings from this period reflect the emotional turmoil of the failed marriage.

**From 1684 onward**, Bada settled permanently in Nanchang, devoting himself entirely to art. He adopted the name **八大山人** (Bada Shanren) and never changed it again. In the mid-1690s, he built a painting studio. He lived frugally:

> "以菲薄之资，居南昌城南小民居" (With scanty earnings, he lived in a small residential quarter in the southern part of Nanchang.)

### Artistic Peak and Most Prolific Period

**167 out of 179 surviving works** were produced between **1684 and 1705** -- during his sixties and seventies. The period breaks down as follows:

- **1681-1684**: Emotional paintings reflecting unhappy second marriage; expanded subjects to include birds and fish; earliest surviving landscapes
- **1684-1690**: Most frequently painted **myna birds, lotuses, and rocks**; angular brushwork; political/emotional expression at its most intense; the "white-eyed" creatures emerge
- **1690-1694**: Shifted attention to **fish**, often depicted alone in vast empty space; achieved his mature calligraphic style by 1689; began studying Wei/Jin calligraphers (Wang Xizhi)
- **1693-1705**: **Landscapes** became a major subject; his greatest artistic maturity; reduced anger, greater serenity; "at peace with himself"

### Final Years and Death (c.1700-1705)

In his final years, Bada lived as a hermit in Nanchang, seeking "solitude and harmony with the natural order." His landscapes from this period bear "little anger" -- the rage of his middle period had transmuted into something more contemplative.

The famous meeting with biographer Shao Changheng occurred in **1690** (Kangxi 29th year). Shao went to meet Bada at the Beilan Temple through the monk Danxue (澹雪). Despite a rainstorm, Bada arrived early. When they met:

> "互相凝视，握手大笑，像是老朋友见面一样" (They gazed at each other, clasped hands and laughed heartily, as if old friends reuniting.)

Bada still did not speak, but "痒不自禁，辄作手语" (unable to contain himself, he made hand gestures). When gestures were insufficient, he took out a brush and wrote his meaning. They talked by candlelight until the candles burned out.

**Bada Shanren died in 1705** (Kangxi 44th year), at age 79-80, at **Baihua Islet** (百花洲) in Nanchang.

## 2. His Names and Seals

### The Name System

Bada used over **twenty different names and aliases** across his life, each reflecting a stage of his identity. He **never signed works as "Zhu Da"** -- the birth name disappeared when the Ming fell.

### Complete Name Chronology

| Period | Name | Chinese | Meaning/Notes |
|--------|------|---------|---------------|
| Birth | Zhu Tongxu | 朱统𨨗 | Birth name |
| Common name | Zhu Da | 朱耷 | Alternative name ("drooping ears") |
| Buddhist name (1648-1680) | Chuanqi | 传綮 | Dharma name, used 26 years |
| Style name | Ren'an | 刃庵 | Courtesy name during monastic period |
| Buddhist name | Fajue | 法堀 | Religious name |
| From age 41 to 55 | Xuege | 雪个 | "Snow individual" -- used alongside Geshan |
| From age 46 to 59 | Geshan | 个山 | "Individual mountain" |
| Age 56-58 | Geshanlu / Lu | 个山驴 / 驴 | "Individual Mountain Donkey" / "Donkey" -- self-deprecating |
| Same period | Luruo / Lurenwu | 驴屋 / 人屋 | "Donkey's shelter" / "Man's shelter" |
| Other names | Daolang | 道朗 | Daoist name |
| Other names | Liangyu | 良月 | "Good moon" |
| Other names | Pengzu | 彭祖 | The Chinese Methuselah |
| Other names | Chunhan | 纯汉 | "Pure Han" -- ethnic statement |
| Other names | Qixue Naji | 綮雪衲 | Combining two of his names |
| From age 59 until death | **八大山人** | **Bada Shanren** | Final and most famous name |

### The "Donkey" Names (1680-1684)

After leaving the priesthood, many of his new names contained the word **驴** (lu, "donkey") -- a self-deprecating term for a Buddhist monk who had left the order. This reflects his complex feelings about abandoning monastic life.

### The Famous 八大山人 Signature: "Crying and Laughing"

This is the most celebrated aspect of his identity. When writing his name **八大山人** vertically in connected cursive script:

- **八大** (eight great) written together resembles the character **哭** (cry) or **笑** (laugh)
- **山人** (mountain person) written together resembles the character **之** (a grammatical particle meaning "of it")

Thus his signature visually reads as either **"哭之"** (crying over it) or **"笑之"** (laughing at it) -- or both simultaneously. This ambiguity was entirely deliberate:

> "哭笑不得之意" (Expressing the feeling of being unable to cry or laugh -- a state beyond tears or laughter.)

This visual pun on every painting he signed was a continuous, encoded statement of grief and defiance about the fall of the Ming.

### Origin of the Name "八大山人"

Multiple theories exist:
1. From the **八大人觉经** (Sutra of the Eight Great Human Realizations, *Ba Da Ren Jue Jing*) -- a Buddhist text also associated with Yuan painter Zhao Mengfu
2. Bada himself explained: **"八大者，四方四隅，皆我为大，而无大于我也"** (The Eight Greats: the four cardinal and four ordinal directions -- in all of them I am great, and nothing surpasses me.) -- an astonishing statement of pride from a man living in hiding.

### Known Seals

His seals changed with his names. Key seals include:
- **Chuanqi** (传綮) -- earliest period
- **Ren'an** (刃庵) -- monastic period
- **Geshan** (个山) -- transitional period
- **Bada Shanren** (八大山人) -- final period
- **Shebi** (涉笔) -- appears on late works
- **何园** (He Yuan) -- garden name seal
- A mysterious seal possibly reading as an ideogram for "mountain" (山)

## 3. Surviving Works and Their Dates

### Overview

Fewer than **200 dated works** survive from a nearly half-century painting career. **179 works** are documented in the standard catalogues, with **167 produced between 1684 and 1705.** Only **11 works** survive from his monastic years (1659-1678).

### Major Dated Works -- Chronological

#### Monastic Period (1659-1678)
- **Lotus and Birds** (荷花鸟, 1659) -- 182 x 98 cm, xuan paper, ink. His earliest documented work.
- **Lotus album** (c. 1665) -- 8 leaves, ink on paper, 25.4 x 33.6 cm. Bears seals of Buddhist names Chuanqi, Ren'an, Fajue. Asymmetrical composition with lotus stalks diagonal, empty void at center. Now at Freer Gallery.
- **Ink Flowers handscroll** (墨花图卷, 1666) -- brushstrokes rendered like kaishu diagonal strokes
- **Flower Studies album** (花卉册)
- **Vegetable and Fruits handscroll**

#### Transitional Period
- **Portrait of Geshan** (个山小像, 1674) -- painted by Huang Anping (黄安平), with Bada's own inscription: "时年四十有九" (at age forty-nine). Contains multiple self-inscribed poems.
  - Self-inscription on portrait: "生在曹洞临济有，穿过临济曹洞有。洞曹临济两俱非，嬴嬴然若丧家之狗。还识得此人么？" (Born in Caodong and Linji, passed through Linji and Caodong. Both Caodong and Linji are wrong -- emaciated like a homeless dog. Do you still recognize this person?)
  - Another inscription: "没毛驴，初生兔。剺破面门，手足无措。" (A hairless donkey, a newborn rabbit. Ripping open the face, hands and feet at a loss.)

#### Huangting neijing jing (黄庭内景经, 1684)
Calligraphy of the Daoist "Scripture of Inner Radiances of Yellow Court." **Earliest work bearing the Bada Shanren signature.** Colophon dated "first day of seventh lunar month, jiazi year" (August 11, 1684). Acknowledges Wang Xizhi's script tradition.

#### 1680s Works
- **Lotus and Rock hanging scroll** (荷石图, 1686) -- one of his rare colored works
- **Birds, Flowers, Insects and Fish album** (花鸟虫鱼图册, c. 1688-89) -- first genuine album with Bada Shanren signature; includes calligraphy by monk Fayi; features the stark "single-eye" fish. Now at Freer Gallery (purchased 1955).
- **Bamboo album leaf** (竹, 1689) -- rare colored work
- **Lilac** (丁香, c. 1690) -- two album leaves, rare color work, 20.1 x 14.6 cm. Inscription: "imitating the painting style of Bao Shan" (Lu Zhi, 1496-1576)

#### 1690s Works -- The Peak
- **Falling Flower** (落花, c. 1692) -- album leaf, ink on paper, 22.5 x 28.5 cm. Dominated by two bold ideograms for "sheshi" (舍事, working things through).
- **Two Mynas on a Rock** (双鹆图, 1692) -- sold at Sotheby's for nearly $3 million
- **Four Landscapes** (山水四帧, c. 1693-96) -- from Combined Album of Painting and Calligraphy. Abstract calligraphic brushwork, debt to 10th-century master Dong Yuan.
- **安晚帖 / An Wan Album** (1694) -- 22 leaves, ink on paper, 31.8 x 27.9 cm. Created when nearly 70 years old. Features the famous mandarin fish with white eyes. Now at **Izumi Hakugokan Museum** (泉屋博古馆), Japan.
  - Fish poem inscription: "左右此何水，名之曰曲河。更求渊注处，料得晚霞多。" (Left and right, what is this water? It is named Qu'e. I go on seeking the place where the source enters; perhaps there will be many beautiful clouds at sunset.)
- **Lotus and Ducks** (荷花双凫图, c. 1696) -- hanging scroll, ink on paper, 185 x 95.8 cm. Exceptionally tall lotus stalks, threatening rock face, two ducks with emphasized white eyes signifying anger. Now at **Freer Gallery** (F1998.45).
- **河上花图卷 / Flowers on the River** (1697) -- **THE MASTERPIECE.** Handscroll, ink on paper, 47 x 1292.5 cm (nearly 13 meters long). Painted at age 72. Depicts the full lifespan of lotus flowers from seedling to bloom. Concludes with a 37-line poem **河上花歌** (Ode of Flowers on the River) in running script -- a dialogue with the poet Li Bai about lotus flowers. Now at **Tianjin Museum** (天津博物馆).
- **Copies of Ancient Landscape Paintings** (c. 1697) -- exact copy of mentor Dong Qichang's now-lost album
- **Landscapes, Flowers, Birds and Rocks album** (山水花鸟册, 1698) -- 17 leaves

#### Late Works (1699-1705)
- **Poem by Geng Wei in Cursive Script** (耿湋诗, c. 1699) -- hanging scroll, ink on paper, 154.8 x 75.1 cm. 12-line Tang poem in cursive script. "Strong and forceful script, each ideogram individually realised." Freer Gallery.
- **Landscape album** (山水册, 1699) -- 12 leaves, ink and color on paper, 18.7 x 23.2 cm. References Ni Zan and Dong Qichang. Completed at winter solstice 1699. Now at **Metropolitan Museum of Art** (1989.363.138a-l).
- **Two Geese** (双雁, c. 1700) -- hanging scroll, ink on paper, 184.1 x 90.6 cm. One goose alert, one asleep. Suggests companionship in a troubled world; alludes to travel, separation, political exile.
- **Peonies** (牡丹, c. 1699-1700)
- **Two Eagles** (双鹰图, 1702) -- "proud defiance of Manchu Qing rule... an expression of brave confrontation and unfaltering loyalty." Metropolitan Museum of Art.
- **Stag and Pine** (松鹿图, 1702) -- Hunan Museum
- **Bird and Rock** (鸟石图, 1702)
- **Cat and Stone** (猫石图, age 71) -- Palace Museum, Beijing

### The Solitary Bird (孤禽图)
Undated but from his mature period. Hanging scroll, ink on paper, 103.5 x 44 cm. A single water bird stands on one leg in the lower-center of a vast empty composition, eyes gazing upward with the "white-eye" expression. Sold at auction on **December 11, 2010 for 62,720,000 yuan** (~$6.27 million USD), making it one of the most expensive bird paintings in history. Seals: "Bada Shanren," "Shebi," and "Puziitang."

### Calligraphy Works
- **黄庭内景经** (Scripture of Inner Radiances of Yellow Court, 1684) -- earliest Bada Shanren signed work
- **临兰亭序轴** (Copy of Preface to the Orchid Pavilion)
- **临"临河叙"四屏** (Copy of "Riverside Preface" in four panels)
- **Praising the Virtue of Wine** (酒德颂) -- after Huang Tingjian
- **Poem by Geng Wei in Cursive Script** (c. 1699)
- Numerous calligraphic inscriptions accompanying paintings

### Surviving Letters
- **Letters to Fang Shiguan** (致方士琯书札, c. 1688-1705) -- Album of 10 leaves, ink on patterned and plain paper. Now at **Metropolitan Museum of Art** (1982.458a-e). Topics: his health, meetings with Fang, exchanges of gifts, and painting/selling works. Gift of John M. Crawford Jr., 1982.
- **Letter by Li Zhi** (copied by Bada) -- also at the Met.
- Shitao's letter to Bada requesting a painting for his studio -- at **Princeton University Art Museum** (formerly Zhang Daqian collection).

### Record Auction Prices
- **竹石鸳鸯** (Bamboo, Rock and Mandarin Ducks) -- sold for **118.7 million yuan** (~$17 million USD) at Xiling Yinshe in 2010
- **Seventeen Works: Nature and Calligraphy** -- **$10.8 million USD** at China Guardian Auctions, Beijing, 2020
- **孤禽图** (Solitary Bird) -- **62.72 million yuan** at Beijing auction, December 2010

## 4. His Artistic Philosophy

### What He Actually Said About Art

Bada's artistic statements come primarily from painting inscriptions, colophons, and recorded conversations. Key statements:

**On the relationship between poetry and painting:**
> "诗者，行止也；画者，虚实也。" -- quoting the Tang poet Sun Ti: "Poetry describes going forth and staying put; paintings express both the empty and the full."

**On originality:**
> "Originality! I am as I am, I paint as I paint. I have no method, I do not think about originality, I am just me." (Recorded statement, possibly apocryphal but consistent with his philosophy.)

**On the purpose of every brushstroke:**
His aesthetic has been described as: "笔笔派上用场" (Every brushstroke serves a purpose) -- likened to "天网恢恢，疏而不漏" (The heavenly net is vast; sparse yet nothing escapes it.)

**On the essence of art:**
> "The essence of art should not be judged by likeness to an object, but by the emotions and intelligence the art embodies."

### Negative Space / Emptiness (留白)

Bada Shanren is the **supreme master of negative space** in Chinese painting. His philosophy of emptiness operates on multiple levels:

1. **Buddhist void (空 / kong)**: Reflecting his decades of Chan Buddhist training -- the idea that everything is illusion, and what each person perceives as reality is only their own version.

2. **Compositional principle**: In most early works, subjects are only partially represented at opposite corners of the canvas, with the majority left empty. The void is not absence but presence -- "the empty and the full" (虚实) are equally real.

3. **Emotional expression**: The vast white space surrounding a solitary bird or fish creates a sense of cosmic loneliness and existential isolation that directly mirrors his own condition as a displaced prince.

4. **Aesthetic principle -- "shuhua tongyuan" (书画同源)**: Calligraphy and painting share the same origin. His paintings are essentially calligraphic -- the brush moves through space the same way, and the negative space in his paintings functions like silence in music.

His art has been described as creating "an artistic realm characterized by emptiness (空), simplicity (简), and distance (远), imbued with Zen sensibilities."

### The White-Eyed Fish and Birds

This is Bada's most famous and most misunderstood motif.

**What it looks like**: His fish and birds have their **pupils rolled upward**, showing the whites of their eyes (白眼). The eyes are rendered as a circle with a dot placed at the very top -- creating a creature that seems to be glaring, disdaining, or refusing to look at the viewer.

**What it means**:

1. **"白眼看人" (Looking at people with white eyes)**: In Chinese, this means to look at someone with contempt or disdain. The expression comes from **Ruan Ji** (阮籍, 210-263 CE), one of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove, who was said to show his white eyes to vulgar visitors and his dark eyes (pupils) only to those he respected.

2. **Political defiance**: The fish and birds refuse to look at the world created by the Qing conquerors. They look upward -- perhaps toward the vanished Ming heavens. As one scholar noted: "透过翻白的鱼眼，宣泄心中对时局的不忿" (Through the inverted fish eyes, he vents his inner resentment at the state of affairs.)

3. **Symbolic animals**: According to Five Elements theory, the phoenix (representing fire/birds) opposes metal (the Qing Dynasty, which was called "Later Gold" / 后金). His birds thus represent a cosmic opposition to Qing rule.

4. **Existential absurdity**: The creatures are also simply strange, comic, defiant in a way that goes beyond politics. They stand on one leg, hunch their backs, arch their necks -- they are individuals refusing to conform.

### His Views on Copying the Ancients vs. Originality

Bada had a sophisticated relationship with tradition. He:

- **Extensively copied** past masters: Wang Xizhi, Ouyang Xun, Dong Qichang, Huang Tingjian, Ni Zan, Dong Yuan, Shen Zhou, Xu Wei
- **Transformed** every model he studied -- his copies are always recognizably his own
- Made an exact copy of Dong Qichang's now-lost album (c. 1697), faithfully reproducing brushwork and compositions while identifying himself as the actual artist
- Inscribed on the Lilac painting that he was "imitating the painting style of Bao Shan" (Lu Zhi) -- but the result is distinctly Bada

His position: tradition is the ground from which individuality grows. You cannot be original without first absorbing the ancients, but absorption must lead to transformation, not imitation.

### Calligraphic Style and Influences

Bada's calligraphy evolved through distinct phases:

1. **Early monastic period**: Studied Tang and Song calligraphers. Used a wide range of scripts: standard (楷书 kaishu), fully cursive (草书 caoshu), and semi-cursive (行书 xingshu).
2. **Standard script**: Modeled on **Ouyang Xun's** (欧阳询, 557-641) precisely executed kaishu.
3. **Cursive and semi-cursive**: Emulated **Dong Qichang's** (董其昌, 1555-1636) swift, fluid strokes; achieved the "flying white" (飞白) calligraphic effect.
4. **Late monastic period**: Explored **Huang Tingjian's** (黄庭坚) exaggerated cursive script.
5. **Post-1680**: Copied Huang Tingjian's "Praising the Virtue of Wine" but in a combinative style -- Huang's attenuated horizontals with Ouyang Xun's sharp corners.
6. **By 1689**: Developed his own mature style.
7. **From 1690**: Studied **Wang Xizhi** (王羲之) and Wei/Jin calligraphers. Brushstrokes became rounded and centered, departing from earlier sharp strokes.
8. **Final years**: Continued studying Wang Xizhi and the monk **Huaisu** (怀素, c. 725-799), master of "wild cursive" (狂草).

His mature calligraphy is characterized by: extreme economy, characters that are "individually realized" and "only occasionally linked," and a unique integration of painting and calligraphy where the same brush logic governs both.

## 5. His Relationship with Other Artists

### Shitao (石涛, 1642-1707) -- The Relationship

**They never met in person.** This is one of the most poignant facts about both artists. Despite being:
- Both Ming imperial descendants (Shitao was Zhu Ruoji 朱若极)
- Both among the "Four Great Painting Monks of Early Qing" (清初四大画僧)
- Active during overlapping years
- Mutually admiring

They lived in different cities: Bada in Nanchang, Shitao in Yangzhou.

> "八大山人在南昌，石涛在扬州...两人毕生未曾谋面，以书信传递往来交流" (Bada in Nanchang, Shitao in Yangzhou... throughout their lives they never met face to face, communicating only through letters.)

**Timeline of their relationship:**
- **1694**: Shitao first mentioned Bada in writing, describing his art as "淋漓奇古" (magnificently strange and ancient). They had not yet made contact.
- **Early 1697**: Shitao actually believed Bada had already died ("淋漓仙去").
- **1698-1701**: Their period of closest exchange. They exchanged painted works, inscribed each other's paintings, shared students, and Shitao's friends commissioned paintings from Bada.
- Shitao requested a painting from Bada for his new **Da Di Cao Tang** (大涤草堂) studio, and Bada painted a centerpiece for it.
- **After 1700**: They wished to meet in person but never managed it before Bada's death in 1705.

Bada inscribed a poem on Shitao's orchid painting:
> "南北宗开无法说，画图一向泼云烟。如何七十光年纪，梦得兰花淮水边。" (The Southern and Northern schools open up beyond words; paintings have always splashed clouds and mists. How is it that at seventy years of age, I dream of orchids by the Huai River?)

**The Famous Forgery**: In the 1920s, the young **Zhang Daqian** (张大千) fabricated a letter purportedly from Shitao to Bada to authenticate a fake painting. The forgery was exposed in 1961.

### The "Four Monks" (四僧)

Bada was grouped with three other painter-monks of the early Qing:
- **Shitao** (石涛, 1642-1707) -- most closely associated
- **Hongren** (弘仁, 1610-1664) -- Anhui school, cold and pure landscapes
- **Kuncan** (髡残, 1612-1673) -- dense, textured landscapes

### Artists He Studied and Admired

**Primary mentor**: **Dong Qichang** (董其昌, 1555-1636) -- identified as his "greatest artistic inspiration." Though Dong died before Bada was ten, his works and theories profoundly shaped Bada's approach.

**Calligraphic models**: Wang Xizhi, Ouyang Xun, Huang Tingjian, Huaisu
**Painting models**: Dong Yuan (10th century), Ni Zan (元 Yuan), Shen Zhou (明 Ming), Xu Wei (明 Ming), Chen Chun (明 Ming)
**Literary heroes**: Li Bai, Ouyang Xun, Meng Jiao, Sun Ti (all Tang)

### Patrons and Friends

- **Hu Yitang** (胡亦堂) -- Linchuan magistrate who invited him; their relationship ended badly with Bada's breakdown
- **Fang Shiguan** (方士琯/方士管, 1650-c.1711) -- closest friend and painting agent/patron in later years; 10 letters survive at the Met
- **蕙岩先生** (Master Huiyan) -- patron who commissioned the 河上花图
- **Qiu Lian** (裘廉) -- poet friend from the 1670s
- **Monk Danxue** (澹雪) -- Buddhist friend who facilitated the meeting with Shao Changheng
- **Monk Fayi** (法一) -- occasionally collaborated with Bada on album leaves
- **Shao Changheng** (邵长蘅) -- his biographer, met him in 1690

### Influence on Later Artists

Bada's influence on modern Chinese painting is immense:
- **Qi Baishi** (齐白石, 1864-1957) -- deeply studied Bada's works, said "愿为青藤门下走狗" (I would be a dog at the door of Xu Wei); equally revered Bada. Qi Baishi's simplified, expressive brushwork directly descends from Bada.
- **Wu Changshuo** (吴昌硕, 1844-1927) -- wrote a poem on Bada's Lotus and Ducks interpreting the ducks' anger as representing the destruction of the Ming
- **Zhang Daqian** (张大千, 1899-1983) -- deeply studied Bada (and forged his work as a young man); collected his paintings; the Met's landscape album bears Zhang Daqian's collector seals
- **Fu Baoshi** (傅抱石, 1904-1965) -- influenced by Bada's expressionist approach
- **The Eight Eccentrics of Yangzhou** (扬州八怪) -- 18th-century painters who continued Bada's tradition of individuality

## 6. His Relationship with the Fallen Ming Dynasty

### The Core Wound

Bada was not merely sympathetic to the Ming -- he WAS the Ming. As a **ninth-generation descendant of Zhu Yuanzhang's seventeenth son**, he was born a prince. When the Ming fell, he lost everything: his family, his status, his identity, his safety. He was 18 -- old enough to have known what he was, young enough to have his entire life reshaped by the loss.

He spent the rest of his life -- **61 years** -- as a Ming prince living under the dynasty that had murdered his relatives.

### The Concept of "遗民" (Yimin -- "Leftover Subject")

Bada is classified among the **Ming yimin** (明遗民) -- those who remained loyal to the fallen Ming and refused to serve the Qing. But his yimin status was uniquely dangerous: he was not merely a nostalgic scholar but an actual member of the ruling family. The Qing hunted and killed Zhu family members for decades.

### How the Fall Shaped His Art

1. **The hiding**: His entire monastic career was, at some level, a survival strategy. The Buddhist and Daoist identities were spiritual refuges but also political disguises.

2. **The silence**: His refusal to speak -- the "哑" character on his door -- echoes his mute father but also represents the impossibility of speaking truth under occupation.

3. **The signature**: Every painting he signed with "八大山人" was a statement that read "哭之笑之" -- weeping and laughing at the state of the world.

4. **The white-eyed creatures**: His birds and fish are Ming loyalists who refuse to look at the Qing world.

5. **The emptiness**: The vast white space in his paintings has been read as the void left by the vanished dynasty -- a world emptied of meaning.

### Specific Political Works

**孔雀竹石图 (Peacock, Bamboo and Rock)**

Created at age 65, this painting directly satirizes **Song Luo** (宋荦), the Qing governor of Jiangxi. The peacocks have only **three tail feathers** -- mocking the three-eyed court feathers (花翎) worn by Qing officials. The inscription reads:

> "孔雀名花雨竹屏，竹梢强半墨生成。如何了得论三耳，恰是逢春坐二更。"

"三耳" (three ears) mocks officials who are "slaves" good at flattery. "坐二更" (sitting at the second watch) satirizes sycophantic officials who arrived absurdly early to greet the Kangxi Emperor during his Southern Tour. Song Luo's family had served the Ming but quickly submitted to the Qing and prospered -- exactly the kind of betrayal Bada despised.

**安晚帖 fish painting (1694)**

The mandarin fish gazes at a poem alluding to Lake Qu'e and the Daoist concept "曲则全" (to bend is to be preserved whole). The story behind the name Qu'e involves the unrightful dethronement of an emperor -- a transparent metaphor for the fall of the Ming.

**古梅图 (Ancient Plum Tree)**

Inscription: "分付梅花吴道人，幽幽翟翟莫相亲。南山之南北山北，老得焚鱼扫虏尘。"

The final line -- "老得焚鱼扫虏尘" (in old age, burning fish to sweep away the barbarian dust) -- "虏" (barbarian) is an unmistakable reference to the Manchu Qing.

**Lotus and Ducks (c. 1696)**

Wu Changshuo's 1926 poem interprets the ducks' glaring white eyes as representing the destruction of the Ming dynasty.

### His Pride as a Prince

Despite decades of hiding, Bada never fully suppressed his royal identity. His explanation of his own name -- "in all eight directions I am great, and nothing surpasses me" -- is the statement of a prince, not a monk. His Two Eagles (1702), painted at age 76, has been read as "declaring his proud defiance of Manchu Qing rule, transforming conventional symbolism into an expression of brave confrontation and unfaltering loyalty."

After nearly five decades as a yimin, the Asian Art Newspaper noted, "he was finally reconciled to his existence and at peace with himself" -- reflected in his late contemplative landscapes that bear "little anger."

## 7. His Most Famous Works with Descriptions

### 孤禽图 (Solitary Bird / Lone Bird)

**Format**: Hanging scroll, ink on paper, 103.5 x 44 cm
**Date**: Mature period (undated)
**Location**: Private collection (sold at auction for 62.72 million yuan, Dec. 2010)

**Description**: The entire composition depicts only a single water bird positioned in the lower-center of the paper. The vast majority of the picture surface is empty white space. The bird stands on **one leg** (the other suspended), neck contracted, back arched. Its eye is rendered as a circle with a dot at the very top -- the famous "white-eye" expression, gazing upward with a mixture of indignation and pride.

**Significance**: Perhaps the most iconic image in all of Chinese painting. The lone bird -- standing apart, refusing to conform, maintaining its dignity in an empty world -- is the perfect embodiment of Bada's self-image as the last Ming prince. "Refined imagery, exaggerated forms, distinctive expression, ingenious composition, vigorous and expressive brushwork."

### 河上花图 (Flowers on the River / Flowers by the River)

**Format**: Handscroll, ink on paper, 47 x 1292.5 cm (nearly 13 meters / 42 feet)
**Date**: 1697 (丁丑年, 5th to 8th month -- painted over approximately four months)
**Location**: Tianjin Museum (天津博物馆)

**Description**: Bada's supreme masterpiece and the most renowned work in the history of Chinese flower-and-bird painting. Created at age 72, it depicts **the full life cycle of a lotus**: from seedling and bud through blossoming to fading, flowing along a riverscape interspersed with orchids, bamboo, and rocky slopes. The brushwork is majestic and turbulent, with strong rhythmic qualities.

At the end of the scroll, Bada inscribed a **37-line poem in running script** titled **"河上花歌"** (Ode of Flowers on the River), a literary masterpiece in its own right. It takes the form of a poetic dialogue with Li Bai about lotus flowers, employs Buddhist and Daoist allusions, and concludes:

> "东西南北怪底同，朝还并蒂难重陈，至今想见芝山人。" (East and west, south and north -- strangely the same. Morning's twin-stemmed lotus cannot be presented again. To this day I think of the man of Zhi Mountain.)

The colophon records: "蕙岩先生嘱画此卷，自丁丑五月以至六七八月，荷叶荷花落成，戏作河上花歌" (Master Huiyan commissioned this scroll. From the 5th month of the dingchou year through the 6th, 7th, and 8th months, the lotus leaves and flowers were completed, and I playfully composed the Ode of Flowers on the River.)

### 安晚帖 (An Wan Album / Album of Peaceful Old Age)

**Format**: Album of 22 leaves, ink on paper, 31.8 x 27.9 cm each
**Date**: 1694
**Location**: Izumi Hakugokan Museum (泉屋博古馆), Kyoto, Japan (originally Sumitomo collection)

**Description**: Created when nearly 70, this is a representative late-period masterwork. Features sparse compositions, unusual white-eyed birds and fish, refined brushwork, and deep meaning. The 6th leaf is the famous **mandarin fish** (鳜鱼) painting with the Qu'e poem inscription.

"安晚" (An Wan) means "peaceful in old age" -- possibly referencing the legend of Zong Bing (宗炳), or a real place called "安晚堂" (Hall of Peaceful Evening) where the work was created.

### Fish Paintings (鱼)

Bada painted fish obsessively from **1690 to 1694**, often depicting a single fish alone at the center of an empty composition. The fish float in undefined space -- no water is depicted, no ground, no plants. Just a fish, eyes rolled upward in the white-eye expression, suspended in white void.

Key fish works:
- **Fish and Rocks** (鱼石图) -- Metropolitan Museum of Art
- **鱼鸭图** (Fish and Ducks) -- Shanghai Museum
- **鱼乐图** (Happy Fish)
- **莲花鱼乐图卷** (Lotus and Happy Fish handscroll)
- The mandarin fish from the Anwan Album (1694)

### Lotus Paintings (荷花)

The lotus was Bada's lifelong subject -- from his earliest surviving works (1659, 1665) to his greatest masterpiece (河上花图, 1697). In Buddhist symbolism, the lotus represents purity and rebirth (rising clean from muddy water). For Bada, it also represented the Ming spirit surviving in the Qing muck.

Key lotus works:
- **Lotus album** (c. 1665) -- 8 leaves, Freer Gallery
- **Lotus and Rock** (荷石图, 1686) -- rare colored work
- **Lotus and Ducks** (荷花双凫图, c. 1696) -- Freer Gallery
- **河上花图** (1697) -- Tianjin Museum
- **荷塘戏禽图卷** (Lotus Pond with Playful Birds handscroll)

### Landscape Works (山水)

Bada came to landscapes late -- seriously only from 1693, at nearly age 70. But his landscapes are among the most revolutionary in Chinese art history.

Key landscape works:
- **Four Landscapes from Combined Album** (c. 1693-96) -- Freer Gallery
- **Copies of Ancient Landscape Paintings** (c. 1697) -- after Dong Qichang
- **Landscape album** (1699, 12 leaves) -- Metropolitan Museum of Art
- **四帧绢本浅绛山水大屏** (Four Large Landscape Screens on Silk)
- **仿倪瓒山水** (Landscape in the Style of Ni Zan)

Characteristics: spatially ambiguous, abstract calligraphic brushwork, influence from Dong Yuan's rounded rock forms, debt to Ni Zan's sparse compositions and bare trees, and Dong Qichang's simplified forms. But transformed into something personal -- the landscapes feel less like places than like states of mind.

### Calligraphy Works (书法)

- **黄庭内景经** (1684) -- his declaration of the Bada Shanren name
- **Poem by Geng Wei in Cursive Script** (c. 1699) -- his finest surviving calligraphic work
- **酒德颂** (Praising the Virtue of Wine) -- after Huang Tingjian
- **临兰亭序轴** (Copy of Wang Xizhi's Orchid Pavilion Preface)
- **Letters to Fang Shiguan** (c. 1688-1705) -- Metropolitan Museum

## 8. Actual Quotes and Inscriptions

### The Most Famous Poem About Him

By **Zheng Banqiao** (郑板桥), expressing the essence of Bada's art:

> **国破家亡鬓总皤，一囊诗画作头陀。横涂竖抹千千幅，墨点无多泪点多。**
> The nation broken, family ruined, temples all gray; a bag of poems and paintings make a wandering monk. Thousands upon thousands of horizontal strokes and vertical slashes -- few ink dots, many tear dots.

The final line -- **"墨点无多泪点多"** (Few ink dots, many tear dots) -- is the most quoted sentence in all of Chinese art criticism about Bada Shanren.

### His Own Inscriptions

**On the Portrait of Geshan (个山小像, 1674):**

> "生在曹洞临济有，穿过临济曹洞有。洞曹临济两俱非，嬴嬴然若丧家之狗。还识得此人么？"
> Born in Caodong [sect], Linji [sect] also claims me; passed through Linji, Caodong also claims me. Both Caodong and Linji are wrong -- emaciated like a homeless dog. Do you still recognize this person?

(The reference to "丧家之狗" -- a homeless dog -- echoes Confucius, who was once described the same way.)

> "没毛驴，初生兔。剺破面门，手足无措。莫是悲他世上人，到头不识来时路。今朝且喜当行，穿过葛藤露布。咄！"
> A hairless donkey, a newborn rabbit. Ripping open the face, hands and feet at a loss. Is this grieving for the people of this world? In the end, not recognizing the path by which one came. Today, happily walking forward, breaking through the tangled vines. Bah!

**On the Anwan Album fish (1694):**

> "左右此何水，名之曰曲河。更求渊注处，料得晚霞多。"
> Left and right, what is this water? It is named Qu'e. I go on seeking the place where the source enters; perhaps there will be many beautiful clouds at sunset.

**On the Peacock painting (孔雀竹石图):**

> "孔雀名花雨竹屏，竹梢强半墨生成。如何了得论三耳，恰是逢春坐二更。"
> Peacocks, famous flowers, rain, bamboo screens. The bamboo tips are half formed in ink. How can one discuss the "three ears"? Just right for spring, sitting at the second watch.

**On the Ancient Plum painting (古梅图):**

> "分付梅花吴道人，幽幽翟翟莫相亲。南山之南北山北，老得焚鱼扫虏尘。"
> Entrust the plum blossom to the Daoist Wu. In secluded silence, do not seek intimacy. South of the southern mountain, north of the northern mountain -- in old age, burning fish to sweep away the barbarian dust.

**On a lotus painting (花卉册):**

> "东畔荷花出头，西家荷叶比轻舟。妾心如叶花如叶，怪底银河不肯流。"
> The eastern lotus flowers rise above the surface; the western lotus leaves are like light boats. My heart is like a leaf, flowers like leaves -- how strange that the Milky Way refuses to flow.

**On another lotus painting (荷花水鸟图轴):**

> "侧闻双翠鸟，归飞翼已长。日日云无心，那得莲花上。"
> Obliquely hearing the pair of kingfishers, returning in flight with wings already grown. Day after day the clouds are without intention -- how then do they get above the lotus?

**From the 河上花歌 (Ode of Flowers on the River, 1697):**

Full poem (37 lines, selected key passages):

> 河上花，一千叶，六郎买醉无休歇。
> Flowers on the river, a thousand petals; the sixth young man buys drunkenness without cease.

> 万转千回丁六娘，直到牵牛望河北。
> Ten thousand turns and a thousand returns to Lady Ding Six; straight to the Cowherd Star gazing north of the River.

> ...东西南北怪底同，朝还并蒂难重陈，至今想见芝山人。
> East, west, south, north -- strangely all the same. Morning's twin-stemmed lotus cannot be presented again. To this day I long to see the man of Zhi Mountain.

**On a Five-word Regulated Poem (题画诗):**

> "鴒原此高蹈，鸿鹄曷翱翔。下者命隽匹，乐天归草堂。北风岁已晏，秋水人何方。迢递巢云子，渔歌楚竹傍。"
> The wagtail soars high on this plain; how do the great swans fly? Those below are destined as worthy mates; happy as heaven, returning to the thatched hall. The north wind -- the year already late; autumn waters -- where is that person? Far away, the child who nests in clouds, fisherman's song beside Chu bamboo.

**On Shitao's orchid painting:**

> "南北宗开无法说，画图一向泼云烟。如何七十光年纪，梦得兰花淮水边。"
> The Southern and Northern schools open up beyond words; paintings have always splashed clouds and mists. How is it that at seventy years of age, I dream of orchids by the Huai River?

### The Character of His Inscriptions

A contemporary noted: "题跋皆古雅，间杂以隐语，不尽可解" (His inscriptions and colophons are all classical and elegant, interspersed with obscure language, not fully comprehensible.)

His writing combines:
- **Buddhist gong'an (公案)** -- koan-like paradoxes
- **Classical literary allusions** -- often to obscure texts
- **Archaic character variants** -- deliberately difficult
- **Wordplay and hidden meanings** -- accessible only to those with the same background
- **Political code** -- veiled references to the Ming that the Qing censors could not easily prosecute

This deliberate obscurity was both artistic choice and survival strategy. The Qing conducted literary inquisitions (文字狱) that could result in execution for writing perceived as anti-Qing. Bada's cryptic style allowed him to express dangerous thoughts in a form that could be defended as mere poetic obscurity.
