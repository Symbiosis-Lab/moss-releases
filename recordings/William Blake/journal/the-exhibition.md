From 15 May 1809, Blake mounted his own exhibition of sixteen paintings at 28 Broad Street, Golden Square — his brother James's house, where William himself had been born in 1757. He printed a *Descriptive Catalogue* to distribute to visitors; the Catalogue is the principal extant statement of Blake's mature theory of painting.

The Catalogue opens:

> The execution of my Designs, being all in Water-colours, (that is in Fresco) are regularly refused to be exhibited by the Royal Academy, and the British Institution has, this year, followed its example, and has effectually excluded me by this Resolution. I therefore invite those Noblemen and Gentlemen, who are its Subscribers, to inspect what they have excluded; and those who have been told that my Works are but an unscientific and irregular Eccentricity, a Madman's Scrawls, I demand of them to do me the justice to examine before they decide.

The exhibition lasted longer than planned; few visitors came. Robert Hunt of *The Examiner* (17 September 1809) reviewed it as the work of "an unfortunate lunatic, whose personal inoffensiveness secures him from confinement."
